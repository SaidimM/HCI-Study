### 1. GroundWork: Bridging Human and LLM Reasoning Through a Structured Argumentation Model
- **Authors**: Sjoerd Vink, Byungmoo Kim, Michael Behrisch et al. (5 authors)
- **Date**: 2026-11-06 | **Links**: [Abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09840v1) | [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09840v1)
- **Status**: [ ] Unread  |  [ ] Dissected for Social Post

<details>
<summary><b>📖 Abstract / Summary (click to expand)</b></summary>

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into workflows that require reasoning over text documents, yet interaction remains largely conversational. This leaves argumentative structure implicit or embedded in text, making it difficult to maintain, compare, and revise arguments as analyses evolve. We present GroundWork, a mixed-initiative system that makes argument structure a shared, editable state for humans and LLMs. GroundWork operationalizes the Toulmin model of argumentation as a typed graph with explicit operations for human and LLM edits. It exposes this structure through coordinated corpus views and an editable workbench for constructing, comparing, testing, and tracing source-grounded arguments. We evaluate GroundWork through an expert study with four participants spanning consulting, product development, law, and immunology, and three use-case scenarios in intelligence, law, and medicine. Our findings show that GroundWork makes source-grounded arguments inspectable, comparable, and revisable as shared human-LLM state.

### 2. Assessing the Impact of Retrieval-Augmented Generation on Algorithmic Problem-Solving
- **Authors**: Gabriel Souza Baggio, Gabriel Machado Lunardi, Guilherme Medeiros Machado et al. (6 authors)
- **Date**: 2026-11-06 | **Links**: [Abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09791v1) | [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09791v1)
- **Status**: [ ] Unread  |  [ ] Dissected for Social Post

<details>
<summary><b>📖 Abstract / Summary (click to expand)</b></summary>

Competitive programming problems pose distinctive challenges for Large Language Models, as they demand not only proficient code generation but also advanced algorithmic reasoning. This study presents a Retrieval-Augmented Generation approach specifically designed to enhance the capacity of Large Language Models to address such problems. Our approach integrates an embedding-based retrieval mechanism and a similarity search algorithm to identify analogous problems within a knowledge database, employing a dynamically adjusted threshold to mitigate retrieval noise. We conducted an evaluation using the CodeContests dataset, focusing on the test split problems that exhibit similarity to instances from the training split. The experimental framework considered three conditions: (i) problems provided solely with their original descriptions, (ii) problems supplemented with randomly selected examples from the training split, and (iii) problems augmented with similar examples retrieved through the proposed method. In addition, we incorporated a Min-K membership inference analysis to examine whether the generated code solutions were influenced by potential memorization of training data within the evaluated Large Language Models. This complementary perspective enables a deeper understanding of model behavior by distinguishing genuine generalization through retrieval from implicit recall of pretraining samples. Performance was assessed using both pass rates and the CodeForces Elo rating of the problems. The results provide empirical evidence that similarity-based retrieval substantially enhances Large Language Models performance in competitive programming scenarios, offering an scalable framework for improving code generation in algorithmic problem-solving contexts.

### 3. What to Delegate, What to Preserve: Human Preferences for Agentic AI Across Everyday Life and Work
- **Authors**: Sarah Wheeler, Veronica Rammouz, Yaoyao Qian et al. (8 authors)
- **Date**: 2026-11-06 | **Links**: [Abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09730v1) | [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09730v1)
- **Status**: [ ] Unread  |  [ ] Dissected for Social Post

<details>
<summary><b>📖 Abstract / Summary (click to expand)</b></summary>

AI agents can execute multi-step tasks on users' behalf, yet the boundaries of acceptable delegation remain unclear. We report a scenario-based survey of 289 U.S. adults with prior AI experience covering 45 use cases across nine life domains. Each respondent evaluated one scenario per domain, producing repeated measures of automation preference, trust, collaboration, AI leadership, privacy concern, and willingness to pay. Automation preferences varied substantially across domains. Generalized estimating equation models showed that greater trust in the AI agent, desired AI leadership, prior task-specific AI use, and anticipated financial savings were associated with greater automation preference. In contrast, concerns that automation would reduce personal fulfillment predicted lower automation preference. Open-ended responses further revealed a preference for bounded autonomy, with participants often favoring substantial AI execution while retaining human involvement, oversight, or final authority. We argue that delegation is best understood as a configuration of execution, collaboration, leadership, and authority.

</details>

### 4. Entering a New Domain in the Era of Generative AI: An Autoethnography of Solo Game Development as a Bioinformatics Educator
- **Authors**: Hannah Kim
- **Date**: 2026-11-06 | **Links**: [Abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09623v1) | [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09623v1)
- **Status**: [ ] Unread  |  [ ] Dissected for Social Post

<details>
<summary><b>📖 Abstract / Summary (click to expand)</b></summary>

The rapid improvement of generative AI (GenAI) not only increased the efficiency of practice but also empowered individuals with capabilities to explore new domains. However, identity formation in the wake of such empowerment remains understudied. We use an autoethnography to understand identity formation in the context of solo game development as a bioinformatics educator. With the goal of developing a create-your-own-adventure (CYOA) game, we collected data for 73 days through journal entries, interacting with communities of practices. The autoethnographer had continuously reassessed the situation, and closely interacted with communities of practice. Their prior knowledge was used to provide in-game context and to uniquely interpret game development processes. These findings suggest that the autoethnographer made efforts to belong to communities of practice and engaged extensively in sensemaking through the lens of a bioinformatics educator. Although GenAI puts power at hand, attaining a new identity took place at a human pace.

</details>

### 5. DeepRadio: A Human-Agent Workspace for Physical Radio Development
- **Authors**: Jiazhen Lei, Yuxin Sha, Sihan Wang et al. (7 authors)
- **Date**: 2026-11-06 | **Links**: [Abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09418v1) | [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09418v1)
- **Status**: [ ] Unread  |  [ ] Dissected for Social Post

<details>
<summary><b>📖 Abstract / Summary (click to expand)</b></summary>

Agentic programming can automate software-defined radio (SDR) development, but decisions can propagate from software to hardware without sufficient user visibility or control. We present DeepRadio, a human-agent workspace embedded in GNU Radio Companion (GRC) for jointly constructing, verifying, and operating physical radio systems. DeepRadio uses a dynamic and state-driven workflow to coordinate a traceable radio specification, claim-oriented cross-layer verification, and mixed-initiative control. These mechanisms expose decisions, tie conclusions to available evidence, and return control to users before consequential hardware actions. Across seven atomic SDR tasks, DeepRadio completed nearly all runs while satisfying all prescribed software and radio-frequency constraints. In a 20-participant study, more participants achieved a verified physical deployment with DeepRadio than with Codex-assisted GRC. Participants reported stronger support for shared intent and understanding while retaining initiative and control. These results suggest that DeepRadio can make agent-assisted physical radio development more reliable, understandable, and controllable.

</details>

### 6. Uniqueness-Aware Feature Engineering for Representing Humans in Algorithmic Decision Making
- **Authors**: Theresa Elstner, Martin Potthast
- **Date**: 2026-11-06 | **Links**: [Abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09403v1) | [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09403v1)
- **Status**: [ ] Unread  |  [ ] Dissected for Social Post

<details>
<summary><b>📖 Abstract / Summary (click to expand)</b></summary>

Fairness analyses of algorithmic decision systems typically treat the feature representation of individuals as an a priori fixed input. How well a feature representation captures what is actually decision-relevant for different people and their unique backgrounds, however, is typically left unexamined. We address this gap by modeling feature engineering as a stochastic selection process over the pooled characteristics as candidate features for a finite training population. Based on this model, we develop a new framework for uniqueness-aware feature engineering which operates in two layers. The first layer measures population heterogeneity using a multivariate hypergeometric model of feature prevalence. It shows how well an individual is represented by any given input representation and which individual of the population is "least represented" compared to their personal, reference characteristics. The second layer acts on this assessment using set-similarity measures to search for an optimum between representational fit of the population and predictive accuracy. Without assuming prior knowledge about unseen individuals, our framework grants represented humans agency over what counts as decision-relevant in their case, gives deploying institutions accountability through a publishable representation fit report, and provides each person subject to a decision transparency about their personal representation fit. We operationalize our framework as a six-step pipeline and show a proof-of-principle using synthetic data. Our approach offers a blueprint for making algorithmic decision systems accountable for how well they represent individuals during development and in production.

</details>

### 7. Tracybot: Preserving Developer Intent by Tracing Agent-Generated Code to Prompts
- **Authors**: Răzvan Albu, Aliaksei Khval, Maxine Orlova et al. (7 authors)
- **Date**: 2026-11-06 | **Links**: [Abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09395v1) | [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09395v1)
- **Status**: [ ] Unread  |  [ ] Dissected for Social Post

<details>
<summary><b>📖 Abstract / Summary (click to expand)</b></summary>

Large language models (LLMs) are now widely used in software development, and LLM-based coding agents are given increasing autonomy. With coding agents, many design decisions are made in the conversation between the developer and the agent. Typically, the developer starts a conversation with an agent and states their intent, the agent proposes options if needed, and the developer plans the change accordingly. Then, the resulting code is committed. However, in such scenarios, the decisions and the intent behind the changes remain in the conversation, with no link to the code they produced. In this paper, we present Tracybot, an open-source tool that integrates with VS Code, Git, and coding agents to keep track of relevant conversations in an LLM-assisted development process. Tracybot captures each change as a Tasklet which consists of optional planning prompts used to plan a change, together with a build prompt that produces it. Tracybot has two main features: (i) it maps line-level code changes made by coding agents to the Tasklets that produced them, and (ii) it allows the developer to export a Tasklet as a decision record when needed, for example when the decision concerns the architecture of the project. We developed the tool over two iterations of a design science study, while focusing the evaluation on the usability and user-friendliness of the tool. In a focus group (7 participants) and a survey (24 participants), we found that Tracybot was perceived as useful for activities like code review, and most participants found our Tasklet design and presentation to be a clear way to represent the developer's intent. Tool: https://github.com/TracyTeam/tracybot. Video: https://youtu.be/Vg_a1F5eO04.

### 8. Sensed but Not Told: Hidden Physiological Adaptation Shapes LLM Persuasion
- **Authors**: Xiaoyan Wei, Yutong Qu, Yutong Li et al. (6 authors)
- **Date**: 2026-11-06 | **Links**: [Abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09394v1) | [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.09394v1)
- **Status**: [ ] Unread  |  [ ] Dissected for Social Post

<details>
<summary><b>📖 Abstract / Summary (click to expand)</b></summary>

Wearable sensors, including those embedded in VR headsets, can provide LLM-based chatbots with physiological signals that users do not explicitly communicate. We tested whether such hidden input changes persuasion. In a $2\times2$ within-subjects study ($N=24$), a chatbot argued against participants' self-selected opinions with or without turn-level heart-rate and pupil-diameter features; sensing was disclosed halfway through the study. Before disclosure, sensing increased perceived persuasiveness by 1.42 points. Across the study, sensing was associated with a 0.57-point greater shift toward the chatbot's position and 3.20 times the odds of any movement toward it. Persuasiveness ratings were lower after disclosure, although disclosure was confounded with session order. Dialogue logs suggested a more declarative interaction style when sensing was on: chatbot replies contained question marks less often, and conversations contained fewer turns. These findings motivate making biosignal-driven adaptation inspectable and providing a genuinely non-sensed mode.

</details>
